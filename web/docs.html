konfettia guide for people

konfetti is a programming language made of confetti. A program is a scatter of colored paper strips on the ground, inside an outline drawn with a crayon, and running it means walking from strip to strip: the color says what to do, the position says where to go next.

Cheat sheet

Your first program

Five minutes, in the editor. You will make a program of four strips that reads a letter and writes it twice.

  1. Open the editor

    Open konfetti.ax, or, in the konfetti folder, run make serve and open localhost:8137/web/. You see a stone circle in a meadow, a cannon in the middle, and a panel of buttons on the right. The cannon fires once as the page opens.

  2. Fire

    Press Fire or Space two or three more times. Paper strips fly out, flutter down and lie where they land. Each strip is one bit of a program.

  3. Pick four strips

    Find a black , an orange and two white strips. Your pointer is a white glove: press on a strip and drag it to some empty stones, between the cannon and you. Lay them in a row: black, orange, white, white. Put their middles about one strip length apart. Which way a strip points does not matter.

  4. Draw the outline

    Press Crayon or C. Put the crayon down just past the black strip, at the end of the row away from the others, and drag an outline around all four. Let go: the outline closes itself.

    Whatever lies inside the outline is the program. The blue dot where the crayon touched down is the origin. Dark lines with a pale edge now join neighbouring strips, and a dark ring sits on the black one: the walk starts there, because it is the strip nearest the origin. If other strips ended up inside the outline, drag them out.

  5. Run it

    Type hi in the Input box and press Run. A yellow pointer hops from strip to strip. Black reads the h, orange copies it, and each white writes one copy. Output shows hh.

  6. Look inside

    Press Debug or D, then Step or N to run one strip at a time. Each step says what it did in plain words, and the stacks show the numbers the program holds. Then load the cat example from Examples… to see a program that loops.

Want more? Press Learn konfetti or L in the editor for twelve short lessons, or read the same lessons as tutorials under Learn konfetti, further down this page.

The thirteen colors

Each color is one instruction. The program works on a stack: a pile of whole numbers where you can only reach the top. Most colors take numbers off the top and put the answer back.

ColorOpWhat it does
redPUSHPushes 1. A run of reds in a row counts up instead, so n reds push n. -- n
orangeDUPCopies the top number. a -- a a
yellowSWAPSwaps the top two numbers. a b -- b a
greenADDAdds the top two numbers. a b -- a+b
cyanSUBSubtracts the top number from the one below it. a b -- a-b
blueMULMultiplies the top two numbers. a b -- a*b
purpleDIVMODDivides the number below the top by the top. Leaves the whole-number answer, then the remainder on top. Rounds down, so −7 ÷ 2 is −4 remainder 1. Dividing by 0 does nothing. a b -- a/b a%b
pinkBRANCHPops a number and picks the way on: zero goes straightest, positive turns furthest left, negative furthest right. a --
whiteOUTPops a number and writes it as one byte of output. 65 is the letter A. a --
blackINReads one byte of input and pushes it, or pushes −1 when the input has run out. -- c
brownDROPThrows the top number away. a --
silverSTASHMoves the top number from the main stack to the stash. a --
goldFETCHMoves the top number of the stash back to the main stack. -- a

On the ground every strip is one flat, bright color inside the same border: a dark line with a white hairline just inside it. The dark line makes a pale strip stand out on the pale stones, and the white one shows where a dark strip ends, so two black strips lying across each other still look like two.

How to read a b -- a+b: before the strip runs, the top two numbers are a and b, with b on top; afterwards a+b is there instead.

There is a second stack, the stash, for keeping a number out of the way: silver puts it there, gold brings it back. Input and output are bytes, numbers from 0 to 255; text is bytes, one for each letter from a to z or A to Z, digit or sign such as !, and several for other letters, such as é, and for emoji. A strip that needs more numbers than the stack holds does nothing. A konfetti program never crashes; sloppy confetti just misbehaves.

How the walk works

The walk goes from strip to strip. Each strip it lands on runs, then the walk picks the next one. Only three things matter: where each strip's middle is, its color, and which strip lies on top of which. Distances are in strip lengths.

Strips link to near neighbours

The start is the strip nearest the origin

Go straightest

When two choices need the same turn, the nearer one wins, and after that the one listed first in the program text.

Pink picks one of three ways

With only one way on, all three choices are the same way. To compare two numbers, subtract them and then branch: zero means they were equal.

A strip on top covers the one below

A dead end stops the program

When no linked strip is left to go to, the program halts, which means it stops. The last strip still runs. To make a loop, bend the trail back into itself: the walk keeps going round until a pink sends it out.

Reds count

A red right after a red adds 1 to the number on top instead of pushing a new 1. So a run of five reds pushes 5. To push 3 and then 2, put another color between the runs.

Programs as text

Every program can be written down as text, one strip per line: its color, then x and y of its middle, as whole numbers. 1000 is one strip length. # starts a comment. The editor shows this text under Program; files of it end in .kft.

# prints "A" (8*8+1 = 65)
red     1000 0
red     2000 0
...
white  13000 0

The origin is (0, 0). x grows to the right and y away from you, as the editor first shows the stones. Lines go bottom of the pile first, so a later line lies on top of an earlier one.

The three examples

Pick them from Examples… in the editor, or find them in the examples folder.

hello: a straight line

Eight reds push 8. Orange copies it, blue multiplies the two: 64. A red pushes 1 and green adds: 65. White writes 65, which is the letter A. Then the trail ends, and so does the program.

cat: a loop with an exit

cat copies its input to its output. Black reads a byte, orange copies it, red and green add 1 to the copy. Pink takes that copy: any real byte plus 1 is positive, so the walk turns left to white, which writes the byte, and on round to black again. At the end of the input black pushes −1; plus 1 is zero, so pink goes straight on to brown, which drops the −1. Brown is a dead end, and the program stops.

count: a ring with an on-ramp

count makes 100,000,000 and counts it down to zero. It prints nothing; it is the speed test. Ten reds push 10. Three times, orange copies and blue multiplies, which squares the number: 100, 10,000, 100,000,000. Then the ring subtracts 1, copies the result and branches on it: positive turns left, back round the ring; zero goes straight on to brown. That is 400 million steps. The terminal version does them in about half a second.

Layout patterns

  • Spacing. Put middles 1.2 lengths apart on a grid. Then each strip links only to its four straight neighbours; the diagonal ones are 1.7 apart. At exactly 1 length apart, diagonals link too.
  • Numbers. A run of reds is one number. Zero is red, orange, cyan: 1, a copy, subtract. Make big numbers by multiplying, as hello does.
  • Corners. Where the trail bends and there is only one way on, nothing special is needed: the walk follows the bend.
  • Forks. Put a pink where the trail splits. Decide which way is straight on, and put the loop or the exit there to match the sign you will branch on.
  • Endings. End each path in a dead end. Brown makes a tidy last strip.
  • Doing nothing. There is no color for "do nothing". Orange then brown copies and throws away, which changes nothing, even on an empty stack. Use it to pad a trail.
  • Joining a loop. Do not join a loop at a right angle. Arriving from one of the two sides, the walk then has two 90° turns to choose between, and the tie is settled by which is nearer, then by text order. Join from behind instead, so the loop's next strip is always the smaller turn. count does this.
  • Keep exits clear. An exit strip within one and a half lengths of another part of the trail links to it, and the program may never stop.
  • Mind the origin. Moving a whole program changes which strip is nearest the origin, and the first heading. Check the start ring after moving things.

The editor

The editor is a meadow you can walk around in, painted like a watercolor pop-up book: flat painted trees, bushes, rocks, hills and clouds stand in layers around a circle of pale stones, and the grass bends in the wind. The panel on the right holds every control; the bubble at the top left says what to do next and what just happened.

On a phone the panel is a sheet at the bottom of the screen. Its edge shows the end of the output; tap or drag the handle to open it half way or all the way. Crayon, Fire and Run float just above it, with Check while a lesson is open. Each group of controls folds away: tap its title to open or close it.

As an app. konfetti can be installed, to open from its own icon like an app. In Chrome or Edge, press Install on the card that offers it a few seconds after the editor opens, or use the browser's own install button. On an iPhone or iPad, tap Share, then Add to Home Screen. After one visit, the editor also works offline.

As a desktop app. konfetti also comes as an app for macOS, Windows and Linux: the same editor in a window of its own, needing no browser and no internet. Download it from the releases page, under the newest release:

  • macOS 11 or later: the .dmg. Open it and drag Konfetti to Applications.
  • Windows 10 or 11: the -setup.exe. Run it; it installs konfetti for you alone.
  • Linux: the .AppImage, which runs as it is once you let it (chmod +x Konfetti_*.AppImage), or the .deb or .rpm package.

In the app, Docs opens this guide in a second window (closing the editor's window quits the app, guide included), and your theme, Mute and the lessons you have passed are remembered. Save asks where to keep the program, with the name program.kft filled in, and Open… shows the system's own file chooser.

Wind in the app: on Windows and Linux the app cannot tell where you are, so the wind there is always made up, marked (made up: no location). On a Mac, the first time you press Wind, macOS asks whether Konfetti may use your location, and says what for. Say yes and you get the real wind where you are. Choose Don't Allow and the wind is made up, as in a browser that says no. macOS remembers your answer; to change it, open System Settings, Privacy & Security, Location Services, and find Konfetti in the list. If you leave the question unanswered for a minute, the wind is made up that time: answer the question, then press Wind off and on.

The first time you open it, your computer may stop the app, because nobody has paid Apple or Microsoft to vouch for it. If you trust it:

  • macOS: try to open it once, then open System Settings, Privacy & Security, and press Open Anyway. On macOS 14 and older, right-click the app and choose Open instead. In the terminal, xattr -dr com.apple.quarantine /Applications/Konfetti.app does the same.
  • Windows: when the installer shows Windows protected your PC, press More info, then Run anyway.
  • Linux: if the AppImage asks for FUSE, start it with --appimage-extract-and-run. The app draws with WebKitGTK's older renderer, which works everywhere; to try the newer, faster one, start the app with WEBKIT_DISABLE_DMABUF_RENDERER=0 in front of its name.

To build the app yourself, for the computer you are on: with the konfetti folder and Rust, install one tool, then make app. It takes a few minutes the first time. On a Mac the result is src-tauri/target/release/bundle/macos/Konfetti.app.

cargo install tauri-cli --version "^2" --locked
make app

In the meadow

The glove
Over the meadow, your mouse pointer is a white cartoon glove. Press on a strip and drag it to move it; the glove pinches while it carries. Drop a strip on another to cover it. On a touch screen, press a finger on a strip and drag. A touch that just misses still takes the nearest strip within a fingertip, and in a pile the one on top.
Double-click a strip
Flicks it into the air. It lands somewhere nearby. On a touch screen, tap it twice.
Moving the view
Drag the ground to turn around, scroll to zoom, right-drag to slide. On a touch screen, drag with one finger to turn, pinch to zoom and slide two fingers to move.
What the marks mean
The blue line is the crayon outline and the blue dot is the origin. Dark lines with a pale edge join linked strips. A dark ring marks the start. While a program runs, a yellow pointer, outlined in ink, floats over the strip that runs next. The faint curved pencil lines on the ground are only the joins between the stones.

Learn mode

Press Learn konfetti at the top of the panel, or L, for twelve short lessons, from running a first program to writing a loop. A card opens with the list. A tick marks the lessons you have passed, and the first one you have not passed says start here or next. You can do them in any order. The editor remembers which you passed, in this browser. The same lessons are on this page as tutorials 1 to 12.

Picking a lesson clears the ground and lays out its program inside a crayon outline. The spare strips you need lie in a row just outside the outline, and Input holds the text the lesson starts with. Wind, a run and the crayon are stopped first, and the debugger closes. The card says what the lesson is about, what to do, and the goal.

Check my program
Runs the program in the outline on a few inputs and compares what it writes with what it should write. If it is right every time, the cannon fires and the lesson is ticked off. If not, the card shows what it should have written and what it wrote, one box per character, with the first wrong one marked; or it says the program had not stopped after 1,000,000 steps.
Hint
Shows a hint. Press it again for a more direct one. After the last hint it says Show me, which lays out a finished program.
Watch it run
Opens the debugger on the program, paused before its first strip. Press Step or N.
Start over
Lays the lesson's program and spare strips out again.
Next lesson
Appears once the program passes. After the last lesson it says Back to lessons.
Lessons and Leave
Back to the list, or out of learn mode. Leaving keeps the confetti where it is.

In the meadow, every strip of the program gets a label with its number and its instruction. Themes

Four looks for the panel, the hint, the lesson card and the debugger. The meadow stays the same.

  • paper: torn sheets of cream watercolor paper, headings lettered by hand on pale washes. The default.
  • toybox: chunky outlined buttons, like a toy.
  • craft: paper notes held on with tape, in handwriting.
  • arcade: a dark arcade cabinet with neon edges.

Switch with the buttons at the bottom of the panel, or add ?theme=craft (or paper, toybox or arcade) to the address. The editor remembers the theme you last picked with the buttons in this browser.

Sound

The editor makes every sound itself as it plays; there are no sound files. You hear the cannon, each strip landing, strips being picked up and put down, a flick, the crayon scratching as you draw, and the outline closing. While a program runs, every step plays a note. Each color has its own note, all from the same five-note scale, so any program sounds like a tune. Output, the end of a run and mistakes have sounds too. Birds call now and then. With the wind on, you hear it rise and fall with the gusts.

Browsers stay silent until you click or press a key on the page, so the sound starts then. Mute or M turns it all off, and the editor remembers that in this browser. When the tab is hidden, it goes quiet by itself.

Wind and the flag

Press Wind or W to bring in the real wind. Your browser asks for your location (in the desktop app, macOS asks on a Mac, and Windows and Linux have none to give); konfetti rounds it to about 10 km and asks the free weather service Open-Meteo for the wind there right now. The panel shows it, for example 5.7 m/s from 196°: the speed, and the compass direction it blows from. If you say no, or the weather cannot be reached, the editor makes up a wind of 3 to 10 m/s and says so. Press Wind again to stop it.

  • In the air, confetti drifts with the wind: about one strip length a second for every m/s.
  • On the ground, strips only move in gusts, and slowly: about two or three lengths a minute in a 6 m/s wind. They never lift off again. All strips move at nearly the same pace, so a program keeps its shape for a while, but the whole pile creeps out of the crayon outline, which stays put.
  • A strip that moves has new coordinates, so the wind rewrites your program. A run that has started keeps the program as it was when it started.

The flag on the tall pole at the back left of the stone circle shows the wind. With the wind off it hangs limp. As the wind grows it lifts, streams away downwind and ripples faster, so you can read both direction and strength from it. Even with the wind off, the grass and trees sway, the clouds drift and the windmill turns in a light breeze of their own; turn the wind on and they follow the real one.

Save the program before you turn the wind on. Open… puts it back exactly.

In the terminal

The editor is one way to run konfetti; the terminal is another. Building needs Rust with its WebAssembly target, and Python 3 for the editor's little web server.

rustup target add wasm32-unknown-unknown
make          # build konfetti and the editor's runtime
make test     # run the tests
make serve    # start the editor

make serve serves the editor at localhost:8137/web/.

Run a program

./target/release/konfetti examples/hello.kft
echo hi | ./target/release/konfetti examples/cat.kft

The first prints A; the second prints hi. The program reads what you pipe into it. With nothing piped in, it waits for you to type: finish with Ctrl+D. Output goes to the screen.

To save a program from the editor, copy the text under Program into a file ending in .kft.

Watch every step

echo hi | ./target/release/konfetti --trace examples/cat.kft

--trace prints one line per step to the error stream: the strip's number in the file, its color and position, then both stacks, top on the right.

bit 0    black   (0, 1000)  main [104]  stash []
bit 1    orange  (0, 2200)  main [104, 104]  stash []

Make a program into a command

./target/release/konfetti build examples/cat.kft -o cat
echo hi | ./cat

build writes one file that runs your program, about half a megabyte: the konfetti runtime with your program inside. It runs on the same kind of computer as the one that built it, without konfetti installed. It reads and writes like konfetti cat.kft does, and ignores anything you type after its name. A program with a mistake in it fails to build, so you find out straight away.

On a Mac, a built program that was downloaded from the internet is blocked, like any app from an unknown developer. It cannot be signed to get past that: your program sits after the end of the runtime, and Apple's signing tool refuses such a file. If you trust it, this lets it run:

xattr -d com.apple.quarantine ./cat

Make a program into a web page

./target/release/konfetti build examples/cat.kft --html -o cat.html

This writes a single page, about 70 KB, with the runtime and your program inside. Open it in a browser straight from the file: it needs no server and no internet. It has an Input box, Run (or Ctrl+Enter, ⌘+Enter on a Mac, in the input), Stop for programs that run long, the Output (its last 256 KB, after a …, when there is more), and the program itself under The program. Send it to anyone. The editor's Build button makes the same page from the text in its box.

Run a .kft file directly

Make the first line of the file

#!/usr/bin/env konfetti

then chmod +x prog.kft. With konfetti in a folder on your PATH, ./prog.kft runs it. To konfetti the line is just a comment.

When it ends

konfetti exits with 0 when the program halts, 1 when a file cannot be read or written or the program has a mistake, and 2 when the command itself is wrong, after printing how to use it. Mistakes look like this:

konfetti: bad.kft: line 1: unknown color `teal`

Learn konfetti

Seventeen tutorials, to read in order. Each one ends with a program that works: one you ran, changed or made. The first twelve follow the twelve lessons of the editor's learn mode, with the same programs. The next four go further, and design new programs from nothing. The last one is about finding mistakes. A cheat sheet closes the page.

Two ways to follow along

  • In the editor. Press Learn konfetti or L and pick the lesson with the same number. The program is laid out for you, with the spare strips you need lying just outside the outline. Check my program tells you when it works.
  • In the terminal. Every program in the tutorials is a file in the konfetti folder, under lessons or examples. Build konfetti once with make (see In the terminal), then run the commands given, from the konfetti folder. To change a program, copy its file and edit the copy. To see a file in the editor, paste its text into As text (.kft), under Program, and press Load text.

How to read the diagrams

Each diagram is drawn from the program's file. Every strip sits at its real position, seen from above: x grows to the right and y up the page, which is away from you in the editor. The strips are drawn shorter than they are, so that the arrows fit between them.

  • The number on a strip is its line in the file, counting from 0, leaving out comments and blank lines. Traces and the debugger use the same numbers.
  • The blue dot is the origin. The dashed arrow from it is the first heading, and the ring marks the strip the walk starts on.
  • Solid arrows show the walk for the input in the step table. Dashed arrows show ways that only other inputs take.
  • A strip in a dashed frame is one the lesson asks you to add.

How to read the step tables

The tables are made with konfetti --trace, which prints one line per step. Each row is one step: the strip that ran, what it did, and the stacks after it ran, with the top on the right. The stash has a column only where the program uses it. Output is everything written so far. A box like 255 is a byte that is not a letter, digit or sign.

1Follow the trail

You will learn what a program is, where the walk starts, and what black and white strips do.

A konfetti program is a trail of paper strips. Running it means walking along the trail, from strip to strip. Each strip the walk lands on does one small thing, chosen by its color. The walk starts on the strip nearest the origin and stops where the trail ends.

This program has two colors. A black strip reads the next character of the input. A white strip writes a character to the output. The trail goes black, white, black, white, so the program reads a character and writes it, twice.

Each letter from a to z or A to Z, each digit from 0 to 9, and signs such as ! and ? are one character. Other letters, such as é or å, count as several, and so do emoji and some signs, such as €. Black reads them one part at a time.

# Reads two characters and writes each one straight back.
black    1200      0
white    2400      0
black    3600      0

3The stack

You will learn that characters are numbers, what the stack is, and what push and pop mean.

To a computer, text is numbers. Each character has one: a is 97, b is 98, A is 65, a space is 32. The program keeps numbers on a stack: a pile where only the top one can be reached. Putting a number on top is a push; taking the top one off is a pop.

So this is exactly what the two colors do. Black reads one character and pushes its number. White pops the top number and writes it as a character. A number that was pushed last is popped first.

14Counting down

You will learn the difference between a digit and a number, swapping the top two numbers with yellow, a loop that runs a set number of times, keeping a constant in the stash, and joining a ring safely.

The task: read one digit, then write the digits from it down to 1. 5 should give 54321.

Plan

  • A digit is a character, and characters are numbers: 0 is 48, 1 is 49, up to 9, which is 57. So the digit 5 is read as 53, and 53 − 48 gives the number 5. To write a number n from 0 to 9 as a digit, write n + 48.
  • 48 is needed every time round the loop. Make it once (7 × 7 − 1), copy it, and keep the copy in the stash. gold, orange, silver copies the top of the stash onto the main stack and leaves the stash as it was.
  • The other 48 turns the digit into a number. It was made before the digit was read, so it lies under the 53, and cyan subtracts the top number from the one below it. So first yellow swaps the top two numbers (a b -- b a): then 53 lies under 48, and cyan gives 53 − 48 = 5.
  • The loop: copy n, add 48 to the copy, write it, subtract 1 from n, copy, and let pink decide: positive goes round again, zero leaves.

16Reversing the input

You will learn to store any number of values on the stash, to mark where they end, and to run two loops one after the other.

The task: write the whole input backwards. stressed should become desserts.

Plan

  • A stack hands things back in the opposite order, which is exactly reversing. So move every character to the stash as it is read, then fetch them back and write them.
  • When fetching, the program must know when to stop. Put a 0 in the stash first, as a marker. No character you can type is 0, so fetching a 0 means the stash is used up. Zero is red, orange, cyan: 1, a copy, 1 − 1.

17When it does not work

You will learn five mistakes beginners often make, how to spot each one, and how to mend it.

Two tools show what a program really does:

  • Debug mode in the editor (D, then Step or N). Every strip gets a label with its number, covered strips are crossed out, and the trace says in words what each step did and where the walk turned.
  • --trace in the terminal: one line per step, with the strip's number, color and position, and both stacks after it.

In both, compare each step with your plan. The first step that differs is where the mistake is. The broken programs below are in examples/mistakes.

Two runs of red touch

The five rules of walking

  1. Links. Two strips are linked when their middles are at most 1.5 lengths apart (1500).
  2. Start. The walk starts on the strip nearest the origin, heading away from the origin, or toward larger x if the strip lies exactly on the origin. A tie goes to the one listed first.
  3. Step. Run the strip, then move to a linked strip, never the one just left, taking the smallest turn. A tie goes to the nearer strip, then to the one listed first.
  4. Branch. After pink: zero goes straightest, positive takes the leftmost way, negative the rightmost.
  5. Stop. With no way on, the program halts. The last strip still runs.

Piles. A strip less than half a length (500) from a strip listed after it is covered: out of the program. Numbers. n reds in a row push n; any other color ends the run.
